What Does It Take to Build Software in 2026?

Software is becoming easier to produce in some respects. That does not necessarily mean successful software is becoming easier to create. In 2026, the work stretches from choosing a useful problem and engineering a reliable solution to designing the experience, earning trust, finding users, and learning what to change next.

For decades, “building software” could reasonably mean writing, testing, deploying, and maintaining code. Those activities still matter. But the modern software-building process is wider than the code itself.

AI coding assistants, cloud services, open-source packages, managed databases, APIs, automated testing, and increasingly capable development environments can reduce friction in many parts of software production. GitHub reported more than 36 million new developers joining its platform during 2025, bringing the platform to more than 180 million developers. It also reported more than 4.3 million AI-related repositories. These figures do not measure commercial products, but they show how large and active the software-production ecosystem has become.[1]

At the same time, the evidence does not support the simple story that AI has made engineering obsolete. Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey received more than 49,000 responses from 177 countries. Eighty-four percent of respondents said they use or plan to use AI tools in development, while 46% said they distrust the accuracy of AI output. Sixty-six percent were frustrated by AI solutions that were “almost right,” and 45% said debugging AI-generated code can take more time.[2]

So what does it actually take to build software in 2026? The evidence points to a broader answer: technical ability is still foundational, but successful software increasingly depends on connecting engineering with product judgment, user experience, communication, distribution, and continuous learning.

Evidence Note

This article separates survey findings, experimental research, institutional guidance, case-study evidence, and inference. Founder-reported revenue and user figures are identified as such. The research does not establish that marketing or branding is universally more important than engineering; it tests whether the relative bottleneck can shift when software production becomes faster.

Writing Software Is Only One Part of the Process

A software product has to pass through several different problems before it becomes useful:

  1. Identify a problem worth solving.
  2. Translate it into a workable product.
  3. Engineer it reliably and securely.
  4. Make the experience understandable.
  5. Help the right people discover it.
  6. Give them reasons to trust it.
  7. Deliver enough value that they keep using it.
  8. Learn from failures and improve it.

Coding is central to this chain, but it is not the entire chain. A technically correct application can still fail if it solves a weak problem, has poor product-market fit, or never reaches enough potential users.

Evidence: CB Insights' 2026 analysis of 431 VC-backed shutdowns is the main source for the product/market distinction in this section.[3]

CB Insights' March 2026 analysis of 431 VC-backed companies that shut down since 2023 found that 43% cited poor product-market fit, 29% bad timing, and 19% unsustainable unit economics. Multiple causes could apply to one company, and the analysis is based on publicly documented company failures, including post-mortems, founder interviews, and shutdown announcements. Still, it is useful evidence that technical completion and market success are different problems.[3]

The Technical Skills Still Matter

One of the biggest changes visible in software development in 2026 is how much technical work can be assisted, accelerated, or partially delegated to tools.

JetBrains' 2025 Developer Ecosystem Survey, based on 24,534 developers across 194 countries, found that 85% regularly use AI tools for coding and development and 62% rely on at least one AI coding assistant, agent, or AI-enabled editor.[4]

Developers can increasingly delegate boilerplate, documentation, code conversion, searching, and other repetitive work. JetBrains reports that developers are more willing to delegate these tasks while preferring to retain control over creative and complex work such as debugging and application logic. But somebody still has to decide whether the resulting implementation is correct, secure, maintainable, and appropriate for the system.[4]

Generating code is not the same as understanding software

A 2025 randomized controlled trial by METR involved 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 tasks in mature repositories. In that study, allowing early-2025 AI tools made the developers take 19% longer on average. METR cautioned that the result should not be generalized to all developers or all software work.[5]

By February 2026, however, METR reported that its newer evidence suggested developers were likely getting more speedup from AI than they had in early 2025, although selection effects made the size of that improvement difficult to measure reliably. This highlights how quickly the measured effect of AI on software development is changing.[14]

That result does not mean AI is generally bad for programming. It demonstrates something more important: AI's effect depends on the task, codebase, tool, developer, and workflow. Stack Overflow's survey points in the same direction: adoption is high, but trust remains limited.[2]

This makes fundamentals valuable even when less code is typed manually. In practice, developers still need enough understanding of a system to evaluate whether generated code fits its data flow, state management, APIs, error handling, testing, architecture, and other constraints.

Security is still part of building

Software also has to survive contact with the real world. OWASP's 2025 Top 10 includes broken access control, security misconfiguration, software supply-chain failures, cryptographic failures, injection, insecure design, authentication failures, software or data integrity failures, logging and alerting failures, and mishandling exceptional conditions.[6]

NIST's Secure Software Development Framework treats secure development as a set of practices that can be integrated into the software development lifecycle. The December 2025 draft of SSDF 1.2 describes new and improved practices, tasks, and examples for the secure and reliable development, delivery, and improvement of software.[7] AI can assist with security-related development work, but secure development practices still require appropriate human and organizational oversight.

Finding the Right Problem

The next challenge appears before most code is written: deciding what deserves to exist.

Product judgment means identifying a real problem, understanding who experiences it, determining how valuable a solution would be, and deciding which version is worth building. It also means deciding what not to build.

If implementation becomes cheaper, a builder can respond to more ideas by building them. But the ability to build more ideas does not increase the number of problems people are willing to solve. That is why the CB Insights finding on product-market fit matters: its analysis shows that companies can receive substantial investment and still fail to achieve product-market fit.[3]

It is therefore reasonable to infer that faster implementation can make product judgment more consequential. That is an inference, not a measured law of the industry.

Making Software People Want to Use

A working product is not automatically an understandable product.

Users arrive without the developer's mental model. They do not know which controls matter, what an unfamiliar term means, or why a feature exists. They judge the product through the information available to them at each moment.

UX is therefore part of product performance, not merely visual decoration. Nielsen Norman Group's current UX Metrics and ROI report contains 44 real-world case studies showing teams using quantitative UX metrics to evaluate design changes. Importantly, some interventions produced neutral or negative results as well as positive ones. That is evidence for iteration and measurement rather than a simplistic promise that every redesign improves outcomes.[8]

Good UX can reduce unnecessary explanation, make information easier to recognize, prevent some user errors, and make system feedback easier to understand. These are established usability principles rather than claims that every redesign will improve business results. Trust can also be supported by clear feedback and understandable interactions.

Evidence: Nielsen Norman Group's usability heuristics cover visibility of system status, matching users' language, error prevention, recognition rather than recall, error recovery, and help/documentation. Its UX Metrics and ROI report also shows that design changes can have positive, neutral, or negative measured effects.[13] [8]

The Harder Problem: Getting People to Find It

A finished product can still have no audience. This is where building software divides into two different activities: creation and distribution.

Creation asks: “Can we make the thing?” Distribution asks: “How does the right person encounter the thing?”

Distribution can happen through very different channels. In the case studies in this article, the routes include Reddit and community participation, social sharing and word of mouth, and App Store discovery. The correct channel depends on the product and its audience.

Evidence: The distribution examples are supported by the Runpod, AppAlchemy, and Personal Best case studies.[9] [10] [11]

This distinction matters because putting software online solves availability, but it does not automatically solve discovery, comprehension, or trust.

When Everyone Can Build, Attention Becomes Scarce

There is evidence that participation in software development platforms is expanding, although developer growth should not be treated as a direct count of competing products.

GitHub reported more than 36 million new developers joining its platform during 2025 and more than 180 million developers on the platform overall. It also reported record activity across repositories, pull requests, and code pushes.[1]

AI adds another layer. GitHub reported more than 4.3 million AI-related repositories in 2025.[1] These numbers do not prove that marketing has become more important than engineering, or that human attention has objectively become scarcer. They do support a narrower observation: participation in software production is growing, which can make user discovery a more visible constraint.

Is Software Becoming Brand Building?

This is the article's central hypothesis, and the evidence is mixed.

There is a plausible case for it. When many products provide similar functionality, users still need reasons to choose one. A plausible explanation is that reputation can reduce perceived risk, while community and social proof can make an unfamiliar product easier to trust. Consistent communication can also make a product easier to understand.

But brand should not be confused with social-media popularity. Some software can spread because it solves an urgent problem, fits an existing workflow, ranks well in a marketplace, or is recommended by a trusted person. In those cases, utility may be the primary growth mechanism even when the creator has little public identity.

The best way to test the hypothesis is to look at different successful products rather than assuming one growth formula.

Evidence: The following case studies are used as examples, not as representative samples of software companies.[9] [10] [11]

Three Software Success Stories Documented in 2026

Case study 1

Runpod: community distribution can become a business advantage

Runpod is an AI infrastructure platform founded by Pardeep Singh and Zhen Lu. TechCrunch reported in January 2026 that the company had reached a $120 million annual revenue run rate, based on figures supplied by the founders. The company had bootstrapped to more than $24 million in revenue before raising a $20 million seed round after an investor discovered it through Reddit posts. TechCrunch also reported that Hugging Face cofounder Julien Chaumond became an angel investor after using the product and contacting the company through support.[9]

The lesson is not that posting on Reddit guarantees growth. Runpod addressed a real infrastructure problem and spent years developing the product. The case instead shows community functioning as distribution: posts created visibility, the product gave people a reason to try it, and users became part of the company's growth network.

Case study 2

AppAlchemy: fast building was followed by continuous audience work

In a July 2026 Indie Hackers interview, AppAlchemy founder Diego Roshardt reported that his browser-based native-app builder had reached more than $10,000 in monthly recurring revenue about a year after launch. He described building the initial product quickly and then using Reddit, X, customer feedback, and marketing to continue developing it.[10]

The figures are self-reported. The case is nevertheless useful because it illustrates a key distinction: building a first version quickly did not finish the job. Finding users, communicating the product, charging for it, and learning what users wanted became part of the development process.

Case study 3

Personal Best: a counterexample to the “brand first” theory

Personal Best is an iOS workout-tracking application created by software engineer Shaun Donnelly. In a September 2026 Indie Hackers interview, Donnelly reported more than 500,000 downloads, about 50,000 monthly active users, several App Store features, and about $5,000 in monthly recurring revenue. He said the app began as a basic hobby project in 2020 and that he had not formally validated the idea before building it.[11]

These are founder-reported figures. More importantly, the story challenges a brand-first theory. Donnelly says growth was almost entirely organic, accelerated after Apple began featuring the app, and was also supported by product-led sharing and direct user referrals. He does maintain social accounts, but the interview does not present a large personal brand as the main growth mechanism.

What Skills Does a Software Builder Need in 2026?

1. Technical judgment

Programming remains important, along with architecture, debugging, testing, security, performance, data handling, code review, and maintenance. AI increases the amount of implementation that can be generated; it does not eliminate the need to determine whether the implementation is correct.

2. Product judgment

Builders need to identify meaningful problems, understand target users, define requirements, prioritize features, and decide what not to build. Evidence on product-market fit makes this a practical constraint, not just a business-school concept.[3]

3. Design and usability

Builders need to understand how people actually experience the product: interaction design, onboarding, accessibility, information hierarchy, error handling, and unnecessary friction. UX research supports treating design as an iterative activity rather than final decoration.[8]

4. Communication

Someone has to explain what the product does, why it matters, how it works, what it costs, and what users should expect. That includes documentation, onboarding, support, landing pages, release notes, and public communication. NN/g's usability guidance specifically emphasizes understandable language, timely system feedback, and task-focused help and documentation.[13]

5. Marketing

Marketing connects a product to a market. In the AppAlchemy case, the founder describes Reddit and Twitter posting, word of mouth, and an influencer mention as sources of growth. That is one case, not a universal marketing formula.[10]

6. Distribution

Distribution asks where potential users already spend attention and how the product can enter those environments. The three case studies show why the answer varies: Reddit/community for Runpod, Reddit/social sharing for AppAlchemy, and App Store featuring plus product-led sharing for Personal Best.[9] [10] [11]

7. Business judgment

Commercial software also requires pricing, cost awareness, retention, and an understanding of whether the business can support the product. Personal Best, for example, describes repeated pricing and trial-length experiments, while CB Insights identifies unsustainable unit economics as a cited failure cause in its 2026 shutdown analysis.[11] [3]

8. Adaptability

Tools are changing quickly. Stack Overflow found that over 36% of respondents had learned AI-enabled tools for their job or career in the previous year.[12] A modern builder therefore needs to evaluate new tools without blindly trusting them.

Evidence: Stack Overflow's developer survey provides the learning/adaptation statistic; its AI results also show that trust in AI output remains limited.[12] [2]

The Software Builder Is Becoming More Than a Programmer

The evidence supports this statement with an important qualification: the modern builder is not necessarily becoming a marketer instead of a programmer. Rather, the boundary around “building software” is expanding.

A solo developer may use AI to produce a prototype that once required substantially more manual coding. But that creates other questions: Is the problem worth solving? Is the implementation reliable? Is the interface clear? Does the product earn trust? How will users discover it? Why would they choose it? What should change after launch?

In a larger organization, these jobs may be divided among engineers, designers, product managers, researchers, security specialists, marketers, sales teams, and customer-success teams. For an independent creator, several of them may belong to one person.

Evidence: JetBrains' 2025 survey explicitly found developers identifying both technical and non-technical factors as important to performance, while its AI findings show developers delegating repetitive tasks but retaining control over complex work.[4]
The practical shift is not from “coding” to “branding.” It is from a narrow definition of software building toward a full product lifecycle in which engineering is one essential part of a larger system.

The Answer

So, what does it take to build software in 2026?

It takes the ability to turn an uncertain problem into a reliable product and then connect that product with people who can benefit from it.

The technical side is changing quickly. AI assistants, agents, cloud infrastructure, open-source software, managed services, and automated tooling can reduce the effort required for many implementation tasks. Large developer surveys show that these tools are already normal parts of development workflows, while experimental research shows that their benefits depend heavily on context.[2] [5] [14]

What has not disappeared is judgment. Someone still has to decide what should exist, whether it works properly, whether it is safe, whether people can understand it, whether it solves a meaningful problem, and whether the right users can find it.

The original hypothesis—that software is becoming more about brand building than product building—is therefore too simple. The 2026 evidence does not establish that branding has overtaken product development, and the case studies provide counterexamples.

A more defensible conclusion is that the relative bottlenecks are changing.

When implementation becomes faster, it is reasonable to expect other constraints to become more visible. Product judgment can become more consequential. UX can become a stronger differentiator. Distribution can determine whether a technically excellent product is ever discovered. Reputation and community may also help reduce the uncertainty involved in trying something new.

That does not make engineering less important. It makes the rest of the process harder to ignore.

In 2026, building software is increasingly not one task called programming. It is a connected process of problem selection, engineering, design, verification, security, communication, distribution, adoption, and iteration.

The builders who understand the whole chain are better positioned to make decisions across the lifecycle: what to build, how to verify it, how to explain it, where to put it in front of users, and what to change next. That is a synthesis of the evidence in this article, not a directly measured claim about individual builder performance.
