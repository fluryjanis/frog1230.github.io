When Not to Use AI

AI shouldn't be used to replace the parts of a task that you need to learn, judge, or verify yourself. That doesn't mean AI should be avoided altogether. The strongest evidence instead points to a few situations where using it carelessly can create problems: when AI is doing a skill you're supposed to learn yourself, when you cannot judge whether its answer is correct, when you assume it can handle a task just because it handled another one well, when an error could have serious consequences, or when you are giving it information you are not authorized to share. To understand why these boundaries exist, we need to look at what the research actually found.

Why the Answer Is Not Simply "Never Use AI"

The question "what should AI not be used for?" can sound as though there should be a simple list of tasks that AI should never perform.

The research does not support that approach.

In fact, one of the studies considered here found that workers using GPT-4 completed certain tasks faster and produced more tasks with higher-quality solutions. Another study found that students could perform better while AI was available.

The more useful question is what role AI is playing.

Is it helping you do something you already know how to evaluate? Is it replacing a skill you are supposed to learn? Can you recognize when its answer is wrong? Has the system actually demonstrated that it can handle this particular type of task? And what happens if the answer is incorrect?

Those questions lead to five practical boundaries.

1. Don't Outsource a Skill You're Trying to Learn

One of the clearest boundaries concerns learning.

If your goal is to learn how to solve a problem yourself, having AI produce the answer can be different from using AI as a tool that supports the learning process.

A 2025 randomized field experiment by Bastani and colleagues involved nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students. Students using GPT-4 performed better while they had access to AI. But when students in the unrestricted GPT Base condition later took an unaided exam, they performed 17% worse than the control group.

The result was not the same for every form of AI assistance. A learning-oriented GPT Tutor substantially improved performance while AI was available and largely avoided the negative effect on the later unaided exam.

That distinction matters.

Getting an answer and learning how to produce an answer are not necessarily the same thing.

If you are practicing mathematics, writing, programming, or another skill, an answer-generating system can remove some of the work that the practice is supposed to develop. A system designed around learning can play a different role.

Boundary: Don't outsource a skill you are trying to learn.

This does not mean AI should not be used in education. It means that unrestricted answer generation can be counterproductive when it replaces the underlying learning activity.

2. Don't Let AI Replace Judgment You Cannot Evaluate

Another important boundary appears when the AI's output sounds convincing but you do not have enough knowledge to determine whether it is correct.

Large language models can produce plausible-sounding statements that are false or unsupported. Farquhar and colleagues' peer-reviewed Nature research examined this problem, describing these outputs as confabulations.

The practical problem is not simply that an AI system can be wrong.

It is that wrong answers can sometimes look like reasonable answers.

That makes human oversight important—but simply putting a human in front of the AI is not necessarily enough.

Cecil and colleagues examined this issue in five preregistered experiments involving 1,403 participants in a personnel-selection context. Participants sometimes failed to reject incorrect AI recommendations. Making the recommendations more explainable did not reliably eliminate this problem.

These findings point to two separate issues:

  • AI can produce incorrect output.
  • A person overseeing AI can sometimes fail to recognize that the output is incorrect.

So "a human is in the loop" is not automatically a solution.

The human needs to have enough knowledge and judgment to evaluate the AI's work and reject it when necessary.

Boundary: Don't let AI replace judgment that you cannot independently evaluate.

When accuracy matters, an AI answer should not automatically be treated as correct simply because it is detailed, confident, or easy to understand.

3. Don't Assume Capability Transfers Between Tasks

AI performance is not necessarily one general ability that transfers equally to every task.

Dell'Acqua and colleagues' 2026 preregistered randomized field experiment involved 758 knowledge workers. Within GPT-4's tested capability frontier, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, while producing higher-quality solutions.

But the result changed when participants worked on a task outside the tested capability frontier.

On that task, AI users were 19% less likely to produce a correct solution.

The important lesson is not that AI is bad at difficult tasks.

The study does not establish that.

Instead, it shows why performance on one group of tasks does not automatically establish reliability on another.

An AI system might be highly useful for one type of work while requiring much more caution for another.

This is sometimes described as a jagged capability frontier: the system's capabilities do not necessarily form a smooth progression where success at one task tells you exactly how it will perform at another.

Boundary: Don't assume an AI system is reliable at a task simply because it performs well on other tasks.

For important work, task-specific evaluation matters.

4. Higher Consequences Require Stronger Oversight

This boundary is a practical inference from the reliability and oversight evidence above, combined with professional guidance, rather than a universal experimental law.

The same AI mistake can have very different consequences depending on where it occurs.

If an AI gives you a bad recommendation for a low-stakes task, you may be able to notice the mistake and move on.

If an incorrect answer influences a medical, legal, financial, employment, or safety-related decision, the consequences can be much more serious.

That changes how much confidence you should place in an AI output and how much independent oversight is appropriate.

The research discussed above gives reasons to care about whether AI is correct and whether a person can recognize when it is incorrect. The practical implication is that the greater the consequences of an error, the less appropriate it is to treat AI output as something that can simply be accepted without meaningful oversight.

This does not mean AI should never be used in high-consequence fields.

It means the role of AI needs to account for the consequences of being wrong.

Boundary: When mistakes could have serious consequences, use stronger human oversight and do not treat AI output as self-validating.

5. Don't Give AI Information You're Not Authorized to Disclose

Not every boundary around AI comes from cognitive-science experiments.

Privacy and security create a separate category of concern.

The practical rule is straightforward:

Don't enter confidential or sensitive information into an AI system unless you are authorized to do so and understand how that system handles the information.

The important question is not simply whether information is "personal."

Instead, consider the particular system's:

  • data handling practices
  • retention policies
  • access controls
  • organizational controls
  • authorization requirements

For example, having access to confidential information as part of your work does not automatically mean that you are authorized to submit it to an AI service.

This is a privacy, security, and governance boundary, not a finding from the four experimental studies discussed above.

Boundary: Before sharing sensitive information with an AI system, make sure the disclosure is authorized and that the system's data handling is appropriate for that information.

Research Stations

The five boundaries above come from two different evidence categories: experimental research and privacy/security guidance.

Experimental Research

Research Station: Bastani et al. (2025)

Topic: AI and learning

Method: Randomized field experiment.

Sample: Nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students.

Key finding: GPT-4 improved student performance while AI was available, but students in the unrestricted GPT Base condition subsequently performed 17% worse than controls on an unaided exam. A learning-oriented GPT Tutor substantially improved assisted performance and largely avoided the negative effect.

What the study supports: Unrestricted answer-generating AI can be counterproductive when it replaces the underlying learning activity.

Important limitation: The result comes from this specific educational setting and experimental conditions. It does not establish that AI harms learning generally or that AI should not be used for education.

Experimental Research

Research Station: Dell'Acqua et al. (2026)

Topic: Task-specific AI capability

Method: Preregistered randomized field experiment.

Sample: 758 knowledge workers.

Key finding: Within GPT-4's tested capability frontier, AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with higher-quality solutions. On a task outside the tested capability frontier, AI users were 19% less likely to produce a correct solution.

What the study supports: Strong performance on one task does not establish that an AI system will be reliable on another task.

Important limitation: The findings describe GPT-4's tested capability frontier in the study. They do not establish that future AI systems will have the same boundary.

Experimental Research

Research Station: Farquhar et al. (2024)

Topic: False or unsupported AI output

Method: Peer-reviewed Nature research examining LLM confabulations.

Sample: The locked evidence base provided for this article does not specify a sample size, so no sample number is added here.

Key finding: LLMs can produce plausible but false or unsupported outputs.

What the study supports: When accuracy matters, important AI-generated information should not automatically be accepted as correct.

Important limitation: The finding establishes the possibility of false or unsupported outputs; it does not establish that every AI answer is false or that every AI answer must be independently fact-checked.

Experimental Research

Research Station: Cecil et al. (2024)

Topic: Human failure to reject incorrect AI advice

Method: Five preregistered experiments in a personnel-selection context.

Sample: 1,403 participants.

Key finding: Participants sometimes failed to reject incorrect AI recommendations. Making the advice more explainable did not reliably eliminate the problem.

What the study supports: Human oversight is not sufficient merely because a human is present. The person overseeing AI must be capable of evaluating and rejecting incorrect output.

Important limitation: The experiments were conducted in a personnel-selection context. They do not establish that people always overtrust AI or that humans cannot supervise AI.

Privacy / Security Guidance

Privacy and Security Station

This category is different from the four experimental studies above. It is not being presented as experimental evidence about cognition or AI performance.

The practical boundary is that confidential or sensitive information should not be submitted to an AI system unless the user is authorized to disclose it and understands how that particular system handles the information.

The relevant considerations include data handling, retention, access, and organizational controls.

So, What Should AI Not Be Used For?

The evidence does not point to a simple list of subjects that AI should never touch.

Instead, it points to situations where delegation deserves caution.

AI should generally be limited or supervised when it would replace a skill you need to learn, when you cannot reliably evaluate its output, when its capability for the task is uncertain, when mistakes have serious consequences, or when sensitive information should not be disclosed.

That does not mean AI should generally be avoided.

The research shows that AI can be useful and productive. Students can perform better with appropriate AI assistance, and knowledge workers in tasks within the tested capability frontier completed more work faster and with higher-quality solutions.

The important question is not simply "Should I use AI?"

It is:

"What am I asking AI to replace, how well can I evaluate what it produces, and what happens if it is wrong?"

Those questions help distinguish useful delegation from delegation that creates unnecessary risk.
