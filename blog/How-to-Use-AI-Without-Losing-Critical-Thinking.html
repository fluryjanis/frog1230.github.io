How to Use AI Without Losing Critical Thinking

Generative AI can make difficult tasks easier. But an easier task does not necessarily mean more learning or better independent performance. Recent experiments suggest that the way AI is integrated into a task can affect what people learn and how they perform without AI.

```

The question is not simply whether you should use AI. A more useful question is: which parts of the thinking process should remain yours?

Research does not support a simple conclusion that AI either improves or destroys critical thinking. Different studies have found different outcomes depending on the task and the way AI is used.

AI versus no AI is not the whole story.

A useful distinction is whether AI is replacing cognitive work or supporting the person's continued participation in it.

You don't need to reject AI. You need to preserve the part of the task you are trying to learn.

The Problem Isn't AI — It's What You Outsource

In this article, cognitive replacement means using AI to perform a substantial part of the reasoning, generation, or decision that the person would otherwise need to perform. Cognitive scaffolding means using AI while retaining an active role in the relevant thinking.

This distinction provides a useful way to interpret findings from experiments comparing different forms of AI assistance, although the evidence comes from particular tasks rather than every possible use of AI.

Replacement

AI produces the solution and the person mainly accepts or uses the result.

Example: “Solve this problem for me.”

Scaffolding

AI provides information, criticism, alternatives, or feedback while the person continues to perform relevant reasoning and evaluation.

Example: “Here is my reasoning. Identify weaknesses and counterarguments. Don't solve it for me.”

The distinction should not be treated as a perfect binary. A single task can contain both replacement and support. The practical question is which cognitive steps the person still performs.

Think Before You Ask AI

If the task requires you to generate ideas or reason independently, consider making an initial attempt before asking AI for a solution. This is not presented as a universal rule; it is a strategy supported by research in specific learning and creativity contexts.

Wong & Qiu, 2026

Wong and Qiu studied 196 university students performing creative product tasks. Students were assigned to a human-only group, a free-use ChatGPT group, or a regulated-AI group. The regulated group first generated its own ideas and then used ChatGPT to improve, develop, and evaluate those ideas.

On the first task, the free-use AI group produced more creative solutions than the human-only and regulated groups. On a later task completed without AI, however, the regulated group outperformed both other groups, while the free-use group's creativity fell to a level comparable to the human-only group.

That study examined creative problem solving, so it does not establish that “think first” will improve every form of reasoning. What it does provide is direct experimental evidence that the timing and structure of AI assistance can matter for later independent performance in at least one creative-learning context.

Don't always make AI your starting point. Sometimes make your own thinking the starting point.

Give AI Something to React To

A practical extension of the evidence above is to give AI your own starting point when the task involves generating or developing ideas. Instead of asking AI to begin the task from nothing, provide your initial reasoning, ideas, or criteria and ask it to work with them.

Direct evidence

This approach closely matches the regulated condition in Wong and Qiu's experiment: participants generated their own ideas before using ChatGPT to improve, develop, and evaluate them.

A second study provides related evidence about interaction style. Urban et al. analyzed ChatGPT interactions from 77 university students solving a complex creative problem. Higher-performing students tended to use richer context, clearer evaluation criteria, specific feedback, and iterative dialogue. The study found associations between these prompting behaviors and creative problem-solving performance; because it was a process-mining study rather than a randomized intervention, it does not establish that these behaviors caused the higher performance.

Give AI something to react to when your own thinking is part of what the task is meant to develop.

Ask AI to Challenge Your Thinking

Another way to keep yourself involved is to ask AI for counterarguments, weaknesses, or alternative explanations rather than asking it only for a conclusion.

There is some empirical support for this type of structured interaction. Kawai, Ito, and Itabashi reported a university teaching intervention in which students discussed ideas with ChatGPT and used it to generate counterarguments. The study found significant changes in the measured critical-thinking-attitude scale.

Another randomized controlled trial involving 61 first-year engineering students compared a peer-assessment approach using ChatGPT with conventional ChatGPT use. The peer-assessment group showed greater gains on measured critical-thinking, problem-solving, creativity, and knowledge construction outcomes over the eight-week intervention.

These studies do not show that asking AI for criticism automatically makes someone a better critical thinker.

Kawai et al. focused on critical-thinking attitudes, while the Wu et al. study used a particular structured peer-assessment intervention in an engineering course. Neither establishes a universal prompting formula.

The practical principle is therefore narrower: use AI's criticism as material that you have to evaluate, not as an authority whose criticism you automatically accept.

Keep the Judgment Yours

AI-assisted decision making creates a different problem from simply generating text or ideas: people can rely on an AI recommendation even when other information suggests that recommendation should be questioned.

Klingbeil et al., 2024

In an interactive behavioral experiment involving financially risky decisions, participants sometimes followed AI advice that conflicted with contextual information and their own assessments. The study found that merely knowing advice was AI-generated could increase reliance on that advice, and higher trust in the AI adviser was associated with higher reliance.

Lu, Wang, and Yin's three preregistered randomized experiments add an important qualification. Giving people a second opinion alongside AI reduced overreliance but also increased underreliance. When people could choose when to request a second opinion, active solicitation could mitigate overreliance without producing the same increase in underreliance in some conditions.

Together, these findings support a more precise goal than “trust AI” or “distrust AI”: calibrate reliance to the quality of the evidence and the situation.

In practice, that means treating AI output as an input to your judgment, not as the judgment itself.

Use AI for Feedback Instead of Automatic Completion

If you are learning a skill, one evidence-based reason to distinguish assistance from completion is that better performance on the immediate task does not necessarily produce equivalent gains in knowledge or transfer.

Fan et al., 2025

Fan et al. conducted a randomized experiment with 117 university students performing a reading and writing task. The ChatGPT-supported group showed greater improvement in essay scores, but the researchers found no significant difference in knowledge gain and transfer between the groups.

The authors therefore recommend that learners actively engage in evaluation, monitoring, and orientation rather than simply following ChatGPT feedback to complete tasks efficiently.

A useful application is therefore to ask AI for feedback on an attempt you have already made when the underlying skill is what you are trying to develop.

That is a recommendation derived from the evidence, not a claim that every feedback-based interaction will produce better learning.

Don't Outsource the Skill You're Trying to Learn

Research provides several reasons to be cautious about unrestricted AI assistance during learning.

Barcaui, 2025

A randomized controlled trial with 120 undergraduates compared ChatGPT-assisted studying with traditional, non-AI study methods. On a surprise retention test 45 days later, the ChatGPT group scored 57.5% compared with 68.5% for the traditional-learning group (p = .002; d = .68).

This was one study involving undergraduate students learning AI material. It therefore does not establish that all AI use harms retention. This study does provide randomized evidence that ChatGPT-assisted studying can produce different long-term retention outcomes from traditional, non-AI studying under the conditions tested.

Fan et al.'s randomized writing study points in a similar direction: ChatGPT improved immediate essay performance without a corresponding significant difference in knowledge gain and transfer.

The practical implication is straightforward: when the skill itself is the learning objective, be cautious about handing that skill over to AI.

Verify When It Actually Matters

Research on AI-assisted decision making shows that people can sometimes rely on AI even when its recommendation conflicts with available contextual information or their own assessment.

That makes verification particularly important when an AI error could materially affect the decision. It is also consistent with the evidence that additional advice does not automatically solve reliance problems: second opinions can reduce overreliance in some circumstances while also increasing underreliance in others.

A practical rule derived from these findings is to spend more effort checking AI output when:

  • the answer will affect an important decision
  • the information has meaningful consequences if it is wrong
  • the claim depends on specific facts, statistics, or sources
  • you do not have enough knowledge to recognize an obvious error

These are practical risk-management recommendations, not categories directly tested as a single list in the studies above.

When You Shouldn't Use AI

The research does not justify a universal rule that people should stop using AI. It does, however, provide reasons to be cautious when AI is taking over processes that are themselves being learned or evaluated.

For example, independent work can be useful when:

  • you are testing what you can currently do without assistance
  • the independent performance itself is the outcome being measured
  • you are deliberately practicing the skill AI would otherwise perform

This recommendation follows from findings on independent creativity and knowledge retention, but the exact amount of AI use that is appropriate will depend on the task and learning objective.

The Practical Workflow: Think → Attempt → Ask → Challenge → Verify → Decide

This is not a scientifically validated six-step formula. It is a practical synthesis of the findings above: preserve independent thinking where it matters, use AI to provide useful input, and retain human evaluation and decision-making.

1

Think

Identify what you currently believe, know, or would try before asking AI when the task requires your own reasoning.

2

Attempt

Make an initial attempt when independent performance or skill development is part of the goal.

3

Ask

Give AI your reasoning, ideas, context, or criteria when doing so helps keep you involved in the task.

4

Challenge

Ask for alternatives, weaknesses, counterarguments, or feedback that gives you material to evaluate.

5

Verify

Check important AI claims against appropriate evidence when the consequences of an error justify the effort.

6

Decide

Make the final judgment yourself rather than treating AI's output as the final authority.

The evidence does not establish that every task requires all six steps. The workflow is a practical framework for deciding when to preserve independent reasoning, when to use AI support, and when to verify the result.

What the Research Actually Says

The evidence is more nuanced than either “AI destroys critical thinking” or “AI makes people think better.” The strongest conclusions are tied to particular tasks and study conditions.

Supported by experimental evidence

  • ChatGPT can improve immediate task performance without producing equivalent gains in knowledge or transfer in at least one randomized writing study.
  • A regulated “think first, ChatGPT later” approach produced better later independent creative performance than free AI use in Wong and Qiu's experiment.
  • AI advice can produce overreliance in decision-making, including situations where the advice conflicts with contextual information or participants' own assessments.
  • Different structures for AI-supported learning can produce different learning and higher-order-thinking outcomes.

Promising but limited

  • Iterative, context-rich interaction with AI is associated with better creative problem-solving performance in process-mining research.
  • ChatGPT-based discussion and counterargument activities have been associated with improvements in measured critical-thinking attitudes in a university teaching intervention.
  • Structured peer-assessment use of ChatGPT produced stronger measured outcomes than conventional ChatGPT use in one randomized STEM education study.

What the evidence does not establish

  • That AI inherently destroys critical thinking.
  • That AI inherently improves critical thinking.
  • That one prompting strategy works across every task and learner.
  • That asking AI for criticism automatically improves independent reasoning.
  • That a short pause or reflection exercise by itself prevents cognitive offloading.

Where MindShield Fits

MindShield is a practical project built around the principle that some AI requests can replace cognitive work that a person may still need to perform.

Its approach is an application of the framework in this article rather than a finding established by the studies above.

The goal is not to claim that MindShield has been scientifically proven to improve critical thinking. It has not.

The research supports investigating how AI assistance is structured and how much independent reasoning remains in the process. It does not validate MindShield itself.

That distinction is important: a research finding can support a principle without proving that every product based on that principle works.

How to Use AI Without Losing Critical Thinking

The research does not point toward a simple rule to use AI as much as possible or as little as possible.

Instead, several experiments suggest that how AI is integrated into a task can affect immediate performance, learning, independent performance, and reliance on AI.

So when the thinking itself matters, preserve your role in it. Start with your own reasoning when appropriate. Give AI something to react to. Use it to generate alternatives or criticism. Evaluate what it gives you. Verify important information. Then make the final decision yourself.

The goal isn't necessarily to use AI less. It's to avoid outsourcing the thinking that matters.

Think → Attempt → Ask → Challenge → Verify → Decide.

That is not a proven universal formula. It is a practical way to apply what the current evidence can reasonably tell us: preserve the cognitive work you still need to learn, perform, or judge.
