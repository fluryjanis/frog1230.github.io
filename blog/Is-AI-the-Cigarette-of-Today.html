Is AI the Cigarette of Today?

Not really—but the comparison can still teach us something. AI is not equivalent to cigarettes, and there is no evidence that using AI causes harms on the scale of smoking. AI also has real, measurable benefits. But the history of cigarettes can help us think about what happens when a powerful new technology spreads quickly, becomes normal, and raises questions that science and society have not fully answered yet. The important comparison is therefore not “AI is as harmful as cigarettes.” It is “Could society normalize AI before it fully understands the effects of how we use it?”

Why compare AI and cigarettes?

At first, comparing artificial intelligence with cigarettes sounds strange.

One is a collection of technologies that can write, calculate, explain, translate, code, tutor and analyze information. The other is a product that delivers tobacco and nicotine and is now known to cause severe disease.

So this article is not arguing that AI is the new tobacco.

Instead, the comparison is about how societies respond to new technologies and products when adoption, commercial incentives and social acceptance move quickly while scientific understanding is still developing.

There are some genuine similarities.

Both cigarettes and modern AI experienced rapid adoption. Both became deeply integrated into everyday life. Both were associated with useful or desirable qualities. And both raise an important question about whether the consequences of widespread use can be understood as quickly as the technology itself spreads.

But the differences are just as important.

The scientific case against cigarettes eventually became extraordinarily strong. Smoking causes cancer and contributes to cardiovascular, respiratory and other diseases, and the causal evidence accumulated over decades. CDC

AI is nowhere near that kind of evidence base. Research on AI is still relatively young, and its effects depend heavily on the task, the user and the way the system is designed. Some studies find substantial benefits. Others find meaningful costs.

That difference is the key to the entire comparison.

The cigarette story was more complicated than “nobody knew”

A common version of cigarette history goes something like this:

People smoked for decades, nobody knew cigarettes were dangerous, and then the 1964 Surgeon General's report suddenly revealed the truth.

That is not quite what happened.

Cigarette smoking became widespread in the United States during the first half of the twentieth century. Historical research describes the 1930s and 1940s as a period in which smoking became normal for both men and women. By 1965, about 42.4% of American adults smoked. CDC historical data

The reasons were not simply that people suddenly decided to smoke. Technological changes made cigarette manufacturing and distribution easier, while advertising helped turn smoking into a socially desirable behavior.

Cigarettes were advertised using ideas such as attractiveness, independence, sophistication, relaxation and slimness. Some companies even used physicians in advertisements. Gardner and Brandt's historical analysis found that cigarette companies used doctors to reassure consumers about the safety of particular brands during the 1930s through the early 1950s. Gardner & Brandt

At the same time, scientific concerns were already emerging.

In 1950, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill published a landmark study examining the relationship between smoking and lung cancer. That same year, Wynder and Graham published a study involving 684 lung-cancer cases that also implicated smoking. Doll & Hill and Wynder & Graham

More evidence followed.

The important point is that 1964 was not the year scientists first suspected smoking was dangerous. It was a major institutional turning point in which the U.S. Surgeon General's committee synthesized a massive body of evidence and concluded that smoking was a major health hazard and that there was a causal relationship with lung cancer in men. U.S. Surgeon General historical review

In other words, the story was not simply scientific ignorance.

It was a much more complicated process involving emerging evidence, public uncertainty, cultural normalization, commercial interests and institutional response.

That is where the cigarette comparison starts to become useful.

Cigarettes show how normalization can happen before consequences are fully understood

One of the most interesting lessons from cigarette history is how easily a behavior can become ordinary.

Once smoking became widespread, it was no longer a strange or unusual behavior. It appeared in movies, advertisements, workplaces, social gatherings and family life. The fact that millions of people were doing it helped make the behavior feel normal.

Advertising reinforced that normality.

Cigarette marketing did not simply say “buy our product.” It attached smoking to identities people wanted: independence, attractiveness, confidence, sophistication and social acceptance. Historical advertising research

The tobacco industry's response to emerging health concerns also provides a particularly important historical lesson.

Research examining internal tobacco-industry documents found a large gap between some companies' public statements and what their own scientists understood privately. Public statements continued to argue that smoking had not been proven harmful long after internal evidence had raised serious concerns. The same research found evidence that some findings implicating smoking were not publicly disclosed. Cummings, Morley & Hyland

The World Health Organization has also documented decades of tobacco-industry efforts involving lobbying, marketing, media influence and attempts to weaken or delay tobacco-control policies. World Health Organization

This history gives us a useful warning:

A commercially successful product can become culturally normal while important questions about its consequences are still being debated.

But there is an enormous caveat.

That history does not prove that AI companies are behaving like tobacco companies.

There is no evidence presented here that would justify making that claim.

AI is being adopted extremely quickly

This is probably the strongest factual similarity between the two cases.

Generative AI has spread extraordinarily quickly.

A nationally representative U.S. study by Bick, Blandin and Deming found that by late 2024, 45% of Americans aged 18–64 had used generative AI, while 27% of employed respondents had used it for work during the previous week. The researchers found that overall adoption had outpaced the early adoption of the personal computer and the internet. Bick, Blandin & Deming

Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that generative AI reached approximately 53% population adoption within three years, faster than the personal computer or the internet, while organizational AI adoption reached 88% in its surveyed sample. Stanford AI Index 2026

That does not tell us whether AI is good or bad.

It tells us something different:

AI is spreading faster than society can easily study every consequence of its widespread use.

Scientific research is usually slower than product adoption.

A new system can reach millions of people before researchers have enough time to conduct long-term studies on how its use changes behavior.

That does not mean those changes will necessarily be harmful.

It means we should not confuse “widely used” with “fully understood.”

And unlike cigarettes, AI clearly has major benefits

This is where the analogy starts to break down.

There is strong evidence that generative AI can improve human performance on some tasks.

In a preregistered experiment published in Science, Noy and Zhang randomly assigned 453 college-educated professionals to writing tasks with or without ChatGPT. The participants using ChatGPT completed the tasks about 40% faster, while the quality of their work increased by about 18%. Noy & Zhang, 2023

Another major study looked at 5,172 customer-support agents using an AI assistant. Researchers found that AI increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average. The gains were especially large for less experienced workers, although effects varied substantially across workers. Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond

This is important because it prevents an overly simple story.

AI is not merely a harmful exposure whose consequences are being hidden.

It can make people more productive.

There are also circumstances in which AI appears to improve learning.

A randomized controlled trial at Harvard involving 194 undergraduate physics students compared an AI tutor designed around educational principles with active classroom learning. The AI tutoring condition produced larger learning gains while students spent less time on the material. Kestin et al., 2025

The crucial detail is the design.

The researchers did not simply tell students to use an unrestricted chatbot however they wanted. The system was deliberately designed around principles from pedagogy and educational psychology.

That distinction turns out to be extremely important.

The same technology can help learning—or interfere with it

One of the strongest findings in the current AI literature is that the way AI is used can change the outcome dramatically.

A 2025 PNAS field experiment involving nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students tested two different forms of AI assistance.

Students using a standard GPT-4 interface performed much better while they had access to the AI. Their practice grades increased by about 48%.

But there was a problem.

When the AI was removed and students had to solve problems independently, students in the unrestricted GPT-4 group performed about 17% worse than students who had never received the AI assistance.

The researchers also tested a specially designed “GPT Tutor.” This version was built with safeguards intended to support learning rather than simply provide answers. The negative effect on later performance was largely eliminated. Bastani et al., 2025

This is one of the most important studies for understanding the AI debate.

It does not show that AI makes students worse.

It shows something more interesting:

AI can improve performance while it is being used without necessarily improving the underlying skill that produced that performance.

And the study suggests that better design can change that outcome.

That is very different from cigarettes.

A cigarette's central health problem does not disappear because you redesign the advertising or teach people to use it more thoughtfully.

AI is different because the relationship between the human and the technology can itself be redesigned.

This is where cognitive offloading becomes important

Imagine two students solving the same math problem.

Student A asks an AI for the answer, copies the solution and moves on.

Student B attempts the problem first, asks the AI for a hint, checks their reasoning and then solves it themselves.

Both students used AI.

But they did not use it in the same way.

That difference is the basis of the concern about cognitive offloading: allowing a tool to perform mental work that the person might otherwise have practiced themselves.

Research is beginning to find evidence that this can matter.

A 2025 randomized controlled trial involving 120 university students compared ChatGPT-assisted studying with traditional study methods. In a surprise retention test 45 days later, the ChatGPT group scored 57.5%, compared with 68.5% for the traditional-learning group. Read the study

That is a concerning finding.

But it is also a specific finding.

It does not prove that ChatGPT generally damages memory. It tells us that in this particular learning situation, the way ChatGPT was used was associated with poorer later retention.

Other evidence points in the opposite direction when AI is designed as a learning partner.

The OECD's 2026 Digital Education Outlook makes essentially this same distinction: generative AI can improve performance without producing real learning when it simply replaces cognitive work, while AI designed with clear pedagogical goals can support learning and skills. OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026

That is becoming one of the clearest themes in the research:

The question is increasingly not “AI or no AI?” but “What is the AI actually doing for the human?”

What about critical thinking?

This question has received enormous attention, but the evidence needs to be handled carefully.

A 2025 study presented at the CHI Conference surveyed 319 knowledge workers and collected 936 examples of AI use at work. The researchers found that greater confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort, while greater confidence in one's own abilities was associated with more. They also found that AI changed the nature of critical thinking toward activities such as checking information, integrating AI output and supervising tasks. Microsoft Research

That is interesting evidence.

But it is not the same thing as proving that AI causes a permanent decline in critical-thinking ability.

The study relies on people's reports of their own behavior, rather than showing that years of AI use permanently reduced their reasoning abilities.

There is a big difference between:

“People report doing less thinking on some AI-assisted tasks.”

and:

“AI permanently makes people worse thinkers.”

The first has some evidence behind it.

The second has not been established.

Sometimes humans and AI together are not even better

It might seem obvious that combining human intelligence with AI should produce the best of both worlds.

But a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 106 experiments involving human-AI combinations. Across the studies, human-AI combinations performed, on average, worse than the better-performing of the human alone or AI alone. The researchers found that outcomes varied by task, with losses particularly apparent in decision-making tasks and greater gains in some creative tasks. Nature Human Behaviour

Again, this does not mean humans and AI are a bad combination.

It means that simply adding AI does not automatically produce better decisions.

The design of the collaboration matters.

AI is not one behavior

This may be the biggest problem with the cigarette analogy.

Smoking is a relatively specific behavior.

“Using AI” is not.

It could mean asking a chatbot to explain a difficult concept.

It could mean having AI solve your homework.

It could mean using an AI coding assistant.

It could mean asking for feedback on an essay.

It could mean generating an image.

It could mean using an AI medical system as a decision-support tool.

It could mean allowing an autonomous system to make a decision on your behalf.

These uses are not interchangeable.

An AI tutor that asks questions and gives hints is doing something very different from an AI system that simply gives students complete answers.

A coding assistant that helps a programmer understand unfamiliar code is different from one that writes an entire project without the programmer understanding it.

An AI system that helps a doctor compare possibilities is different from an AI system that makes a medical decision without meaningful human review.

So treating “AI” as one single exposure would be like treating every use of a computer as the same activity.

It isn't.

The cigarette analogy also fails on the evidence itself

This is perhaps the most important difference.

The scientific evidence surrounding cigarettes eventually became overwhelming.

Smoking was linked to lung cancer through major epidemiological studies beginning decades before the 1964 Surgeon General's report, and the evidence eventually expanded to cardiovascular, respiratory and other diseases. CDC

The evidence surrounding AI is nowhere near that mature.

Researchers have found measurable effects on productivity, learning, retention, task performance and human-AI collaboration.

But the results vary.

Some are positive.

Some are negative.

Some are mixed.

And many studies are short-term.

We do not yet have decades of longitudinal evidence telling us what happens to a population that routinely uses generative AI throughout education, work and everyday life.

The OECD has emphasized the importance of distinguishing immediate AI-assisted performance from actual human skill development and notes that longer-term evidence remains an important research need. OECD

That uncertainty is real.

But uncertainty cuts both ways.

It does not mean AI will eventually prove harmless.

It also does not mean AI will eventually prove harmful.

We do not know yet.

Commercial incentives are another interesting similarity—but don't go too far

AI has enormous commercial incentives behind it.

The technology is being developed and deployed by companies that have strong reasons to increase adoption, usage and investment.

That is not controversial.

But this is where comparisons to tobacco companies need to be handled very carefully.

The tobacco industry sold a product whose central exposure ultimately proved physically harmful.

AI companies sell systems that can produce both benefits and harms depending on how those systems are built and used.

The distinction is enormous.

There is currently no evidence that would justify saying:

“AI companies are doing what Big Tobacco did.”

The historical tobacco evidence demonstrates that commercial incentives can conflict with public health. It does not establish that the same pattern is occurring in AI.

The useful lesson is more general:

When a technology has enormous commercial value, society should be willing to examine its risks independently rather than assuming that successful products must therefore be safe.

Society may be adapting faster than research

There is another genuine parallel.

AI is spreading into schools, workplaces and daily life while researchers are still trying to determine which patterns of use are beneficial.

That creates a difficult situation.

By the time a long-term study is finished, the technology being studied may have already changed.

And institutions can struggle to keep up.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that more than 80% of U.S. high-school and college students use AI for school-related tasks, while only about half of middle and high schools have AI policies and just 6% of teachers report that those policies are clear. Stanford AI Index

This does not mean institutions are doing nothing.

Regulation is already developing. The European Union's AI Act, for example, is being implemented in stages. European Commission

But there is still a gap between the speed of technological change and the speed at which institutions can understand, test and respond to every new use.

That is not unique to AI.

It is a recurring problem with rapidly changing technology.

Unlike cigarettes, AI can potentially be redesigned

This is perhaps the biggest reason the cigarette analogy should not be taken too literally.

Cigarette history is largely a story about discovering that the product itself was dangerous.

AI presents a different problem.

Researchers can change the system.

They can change how it gives answers.

They can change whether it gives answers at all.

They can build systems that ask users to explain their reasoning.

They can make educational AI provide hints rather than solutions.

They can add verification mechanisms.

They can require human approval.

They can redesign interfaces to discourage unnecessary delegation.

And, importantly, research is already showing that these design choices can affect outcomes.

The Bastani study is a clear example. The standard GPT-4 interface produced a negative post-AI learning effect, while the specially designed tutor largely avoided it. Bastani et al.

That means the future of AI is not necessarily fixed by the properties of today's systems.

The technology can change as we learn.

That is a major difference from the historical cigarette case.

So, is AI the cigarette of today?

No—not in the literal sense.

The scientific evidence does not support saying that AI has cigarette-like health harms.

It does not support saying that AI is inherently harmful.

It does not support saying that people who use AI are inevitably damaging their ability to think.

And it certainly does not prove that AI companies are repeating the history of the tobacco industry.

But that does not make the comparison useless.

The cigarette story can still teach us something about normalization.

A technology can spread quickly.

People can become comfortable with it.

Its immediate benefits can be obvious.

Commercial incentives can encourage further adoption.

And some consequences can be difficult to see because they develop gradually rather than appearing immediately.

That last point is particularly relevant to AI.

A student can get a better answer today while learning less for tomorrow.

A worker can complete a task faster without developing the skill needed to perform it independently.

A person can make a decision more efficiently while becoming more dependent on an external system to make that decision.

None of these outcomes is inevitable.

But they are worth studying.

The more useful question

The most interesting lesson from the cigarette analogy is therefore not:

“Is AI dangerous like cigarettes?”

It is:

“Can a technology become normal faster than society learns how to use it safely?”

That is a much harder question.

And unlike the cigarette analogy itself, it does not assume the answer.

The research so far suggests that AI can produce real benefits. It can increase productivity, support learning and help people perform tasks that would otherwise take more time. Noy & Zhang

The research also suggests that some forms of AI use can interfere with learning, encourage cognitive offloading, or produce weaker independent performance when the technology is removed. Bastani et al.

And the effects are not consistent enough to reduce the entire technology to a single label.

The strongest conclusion available today is therefore a cautious one:

AI is not the cigarette of today.

But the history of cigarettes can remind us that rapid adoption and normalization do not automatically mean that every consequence is already understood.

The goal should not be to reject AI. It should be to understand which uses help people, which uses replace skills that matter, and how AI can be designed so that its benefits do not come at the cost of abilities we still need ourselves.

The cigarette analogy may be useful as a warning about uncertainty, normalization and delayed recognition of risk. It is not evidence that AI has cigarette-like harms.

MindShield

If you want to experiment with using AI while retaining more deliberate control over what you delegate, MindShield is an open-source browser extension designed to encourage users to pause before delegating certain types of cognitive work to AI.

MindShield is a practical tool inspired by the cognitive-offloading question discussed in this article. It is not presented as scientifically proven to prevent cognitive decline or improve critical-thinking ability.
