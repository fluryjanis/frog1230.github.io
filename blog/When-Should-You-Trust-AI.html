When Should You Trust AI?

You should not trust AI simply because it is AI, because it sounds confident, because it explains its answer, or because it performed well somewhere else. Reliance becomes more defensible when the system has demonstrated capability for the task, the human has a meaningful way to evaluate its output, confidence information is calibrated, and AI assistance has actually improved performance in the relevant workflow.

Why the Answer Is Not Simply "Always Trust AI" or "Never Trust AI"

The question "when should you trust AI?" can sound as though there should be a simple rule. Either AI is trustworthy, or it is not.

The research does not support either extreme.

Instead, the evidence points toward appropriate reliance: using AI more when there is evidence that it can perform the task and that its assistance helps, while being more cautious when its capability, reliability, or usefulness is uncertain.

Several different ideas are involved here.

  • AI accuracy is how often an AI system produces a correct result.
  • AI capability is what the system can successfully do for a particular task.
  • Human evaluation is whether the person using AI can meaningfully recognize whether its output is correct.
  • Trust is a person's belief that an AI system is dependable.
  • Reliance is whether the person actually follows the AI's recommendation.
  • Appropriate reliance means relying on AI when doing so is beneficial and withholding reliance when it is not.

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable. An AI can be highly capable at a particular task while a person still relies on it too much. A person can also under-rely on a capable system or fail to benefit from useful assistance.

That leads to a more useful question than "Can I trust AI?"

Has this AI demonstrated that it can do this task, and do I have a good reason to rely on its output here?

Four practical boundaries help answer that question.

1. Does the AI Have Demonstrated Capability for This Task?

The first question is not whether an AI system is generally impressive. It is whether it has demonstrated that it can handle the specific task you are asking it to perform.

Bansal and colleagues' 2019 research examined how people's understanding of an AI system's error boundaries affected human-AI team performance. The important finding was that overall AI accuracy was not the only factor that mattered. Understanding where the AI tended to make mistakes affected when people accepted or overrode its recommendations.

In other words, knowing that a system is accurate overall does not tell you everything about when it should be relied upon.

Dunning, Fischhoff, and Davis reached a related conclusion in a 2024 controlled experiment involving 156 participants. Participants made repeated decisions while receiving recommendations from AI agents with different levels of skill.

Participants benefited from highly skilled AI, but their improvement plateaued because they relied too uncritically on the highly skilled agent. At the same time, they relied too little on lower-skilled AI agents.

That finding is important because it shows that human reliance does not automatically track actual AI capability.

A system can be good enough to deserve substantial reliance without deserving unlimited reliance. Likewise, a weaker system may still provide useful information without receiving the level of reliance that its actual capabilities warrant.

Boundary: Trust AI more when its capability has been demonstrated for the specific task, rather than assuming that general performance transfers everywhere.

The evidence does not establish that modern AI systems have simple, universally predictable error boundaries. The practical lesson is narrower: performance on one task is not automatic evidence of performance on another.

2. Can You Meaningfully Evaluate the Output?

Capability is only part of the problem. You also need to consider whether the human using the AI has a meaningful way to evaluate what it produces.

Bansal and colleagues' findings suggest that understanding where an AI makes mistakes can affect whether people appropriately accept or override its recommendations. That makes the human's ability to recognize relevant failures important to the overall human-AI team.

But there is an important distinction between understanding an AI's explanation and being able to verify its answer.

Fok and Weld's 2024 research synthesis makes this distinction central. Their argument is that explanations are most useful when they help people determine whether an AI recommendation is actually correct. An explanation can make an answer easier to understand or more convincing without necessarily providing a reliable way to establish that the answer is true.

An explanation is not the same thing as evidence that lets you check an answer.

This does not mean humans are incapable of evaluating AI. Kelso and colleagues' 2025 experiments provide an important counterexample. Across two experiments involving 1,092 and 1,809 participants, AI assistance improved people's ability to distinguish correct from incorrect eyewitness lineup identifications in several tested conditions.

The point is therefore not that human oversight always fails.

The point is that human oversight works differently depending on whether the person has a meaningful basis for evaluating the AI's output.

If you can independently assess an answer, compare it against relevant evidence, or otherwise recognize when the system has gone wrong, reliance can be more defensible. If you cannot meaningfully evaluate the output, simply knowing that an AI produced it does not solve that problem.

Boundary: Trust AI more when you have a meaningful way to evaluate its output; do not confuse a convincing explanation with verification.

3. Is the AI's Confidence Actually Meaningful?

Some AI systems provide confidence information alongside their recommendations. That information can influence how much people rely on the system.

But confidence is useful only if it tells you something meaningful about the likelihood that the AI is correct.

Fregosi and colleagues' 2026 study examined this issue with 184 adults solving logic problems while receiving AI recommendations accompanied by different confidence levels.

The study found that well-calibrated AI confidence improved decision accuracy substantially more than poorly calibrated confidence. Poorly calibrated confidence could encourage both over-reliance and under-reliance.

Calibration means that a system's stated confidence corresponds reasonably well to how often it is actually correct. If a system is highly confident when it is frequently wrong, its confidence signal can push people in the wrong direction.

This creates a simple rule worth remembering:

High confidence ≠ high accuracy.

The Fregosi study does not establish that all AI confidence signals are reliable or that confidence information in modern language models works in exactly the same way as the system tested in the experiment.

The narrower lesson is that confidence should increase reliance only when there is evidence that the confidence signal is actually calibrated.

Boundary: Treat AI confidence as useful only when there is evidence that the confidence signal corresponds reasonably well to actual accuracy.

4. Does AI Actually Improve the Human-AI Workflow?

There is another question that can easily get overlooked: Does using the AI actually make the human perform better?

An AI system can produce useful answers without every form of AI assistance improving the overall task. What matters is the performance of the human-AI workflow, not just the capability of the AI in isolation.

Kelso and colleagues' 2025 experiments provide an important example of AI assistance producing a positive result. Participants receiving AI assistance improved their ability to distinguish correct from incorrect eyewitness lineup identifications in several tested conditions.

This is important because it demonstrates that the evidence should not be interpreted as an argument for simply distrusting AI. Under the conditions tested, AI assistance could make people better at the task.

Dunning, Fischhoff, and Davis provide the other side of the picture. Their 2024 experiment found that participants benefited from highly skilled AI, but also relied too uncritically on it. So even when an AI system is genuinely useful, more reliance does not necessarily produce more benefit.

The practical question is therefore not merely:

"Did the AI get the answer right?"

It is also:

"Did using the AI help the human perform the task better?"

That distinction matters because the goal of an AI-assisted workflow is not maximum AI involvement. The goal is better performance under the conditions in which the system is being used.

Boundary: Trust AI more when its use has actually demonstrated a benefit to the human-AI workflow for the relevant task.

Research Stations

The evidence below comes from four primary experimental studies and one supporting research synthesis. Each study answers a narrower question than the broad question "Should AI be trusted?"

Primary Experimental Research

Research Station: Bansal et al. (2019)

Study: Beyond Accuracy: The Role of Mental Models in Human-AI Team Performance

Year: 2019

Method: Controlled human-AI team experiments examining how people's understanding of AI error boundaries affected team performance.

Sample: The locked evidence base does not specify a sample size, so no sample number is provided.

AI/task: Participants worked with AI recommendations in controlled tasks where understanding the AI's strengths and error boundaries could affect whether recommendations were accepted or overridden.

Main finding: Human-AI performance depended on more than the AI's overall accuracy. Understanding where the AI tended to make mistakes affected reliance on its recommendations.

What it supports: Appropriate reliance depends partly on understanding an AI system's demonstrated strengths and weaknesses. Aggregate accuracy is not the whole story.

Important limitation: The experimental tasks were more controlled and constrained than open-ended modern AI use. The study does not establish that modern LLMs have simple or universally predictable error boundaries.

Primary Experimental Research

Research Station: Dunning, Fischhoff & Davis (2024)

Study: When Do Humans Heed AI Agents' Advice? When Should They?

Year: 2024

Method: Controlled experiment in which participants made repeated decisions while receiving recommendations from AI agents with different levels of skill.

Sample: 156 participants.

AI/task: Participants made decisions in a strategic game while receiving recommendations from AI agents with different skill levels.

Main finding: Participants benefited from highly skilled AI, but their improvement plateaued because they relied too uncritically on the highly skilled agent. They also relied too little on lower-skilled AI agents.

What it supports: AI capability matters, but human reliance does not automatically calibrate perfectly to that capability. More reliance is not automatically better reliance.

Important limitation: The experiment involved a strategic game rather than open-ended real-world decision-making. The findings therefore do not directly establish how people will rely on every modern AI or LLM.

Primary Experimental Research

Research Station: Kelso et al. (2025)

Study: AI assistance improves people's ability to distinguish correct from incorrect eyewitness lineup identifications

Year: 2025

Method: Two experiments examining how different forms of AI assistance affected people's evaluation of eyewitness lineup identifications.

Sample: 1,092 participants in Experiment 1; 1,809 participants in Experiment 2.

AI/task: Participants evaluated eyewitness lineup identifications with different forms of AI assistance.

Main finding: AI assistance improved people's ability to distinguish correct from incorrect eyewitness identifications in several tested conditions.

What it supports: AI assistance can sometimes improve human judgment and does not inherently make human decision-making worse. The benefit depends on the task and AI system.

Important limitation: The task was highly specific and the AI systems were designed for that problem. The study does not establish that AI generally improves human judgment or that the result transfers to unrestricted generative AI use.

Primary Experimental Research

Research Station: Fregosi et al. (2026)

Study: Too Sure for Our Own Good: A User Study on AI Confidence and Human Reliance

Year: 2026

Method: Experiment examining human decisions when AI recommendations were accompanied by different confidence levels.

Sample: 184 adults.

AI/task: Participants solved logic problems with AI recommendations accompanied by different confidence levels.

Main finding: Well-calibrated AI confidence improved decision accuracy substantially more than poorly calibrated confidence. Poorly calibrated confidence could encourage both over-reliance and under-reliance, while high AI confidence could increase acceptance of incorrect recommendations.

What it supports: Confidence information can be useful when it is calibrated to actual performance. Confidence should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of correctness.

Important limitation: The experiment involved a specific decision-support task rather than unrestricted generative AI use. It does not establish that all AI confidence signals or LLM confidence estimates are calibrated.

Supporting Research Synthesis

Research Station: Fok & Weld (2024)

Study: In search of verifiability: Explanations rarely enable complementary performance in AI-advised decision making

Year: 2024

Method: Research synthesis/perspective concerning the role of explanations in AI-advised decision-making.

Sample: Not presented here as a primary experimental sample because this source is a synthesis/perspective rather than one of the four primary experiments in this evidence base.

AI/task: The work considers when AI explanations actually help people verify AI recommendations.

Main finding: An explanation can increase understanding or confidence without necessarily giving a person a reliable way to establish whether the AI recommendation is correct.

What it supports: Explanation and verification are different. An explanation is more useful when it helps the human establish whether the recommendation is correct.

Important limitation: This source is a conceptual synthesis rather than a new primary experiment. It should therefore not be presented as independent experimental evidence establishing a universal effect.

Evidence Strength

The strongest conclusions from this evidence base are narrow ones about the conditions actually studied. The evidence becomes weaker when those findings are turned into universal rules about AI.

Strongest Support

The studies provide meaningful experimental evidence that AI capability, human reliance, calibrated confidence, and the design of the human-AI workflow can affect outcomes under the conditions tested.

There is also direct evidence from Kelso et al. that AI assistance can improve human performance in a specific task, providing an important counterweight to the idea that AI should simply be distrusted.

Moderate Generalization

The findings support practical principles such as evaluating AI according to the task it is being used for and distinguishing explanations from actual verification.

These principles are useful beyond the exact experiments, but applying them to a particular AI system still requires judgment about that system and task.

Limited Generalization

The studies do not establish one universal accuracy threshold for trusting AI. They do not establish that every AI system behaves the same way, and they do not directly establish that every modern LLM has the same properties as the systems and tasks tested.

The evidence therefore supports conditional reliance, not a universal formula for when an AI becomes trustworthy.

Claims to Avoid

The evidence is strong enough to support practical boundaries, but not broad claims about AI in general. In particular, the research does not justify statements such as:

  • AI cannot be trusted.
  • AI should always be verified.
  • Humans always overtrust AI.
  • AI is more reliable than humans.
  • AI is less reliable than humans.
  • AI explanations prove that an answer is correct.
  • AI confidence tells you whether an answer is correct.
  • If an AI is accurate, all of its answers are reliable.
  • AI should never be used in high-stakes decisions.
  • AI should always be used only as a second opinion.

Each of these statements goes further than the locked evidence allows.

So, When Should You Trust AI?

There is no single point at which an AI system becomes universally "trustworthy."

The better approach is to ask whether reliance is justified for the particular system, task, and conditions.

The evidence makes reliance more defensible when:

  • the AI has demonstrated capability for the specific task;
  • the human has a meaningful way to evaluate the output;
  • available confidence information has demonstrated calibration; and
  • AI assistance has actually improved performance in the relevant human-AI workflow.

When those conditions are unknown or absent, the case for relying heavily on the AI becomes weaker. That does not mean the system is necessarily bad. It means there is less evidence supporting the level of reliance being placed on it.

The research also shows why the answer cannot simply be "trust AI" or "distrust AI." Highly capable AI can be useful while still receiving too much reliance. Lower-skilled AI can be underused. AI assistance can improve human performance in some tasks. Confidence can help when it is calibrated and mislead when it is not.

The goal, then, is not maximum trust.

It is not maximum skepticism either.

Trust is not something you give an AI once and for all. Appropriate reliance depends on the system, the task, and the conditions under which the AI is being used.
