When Should You Trust AI?

You should not trust AI simply because it is AI, because it sounds confident, because it explains its answer, or because it performed well somewhere else. Reliance becomes more defensible when the system has demonstrated capability for the task, the human has a meaningful way to evaluate its output, confidence information is calibrated, and AI assistance has actually improved performance in the relevant workflow.

Introduction: Why the Answer Is Not Simply "Always Trust AI" or "Never Trust AI"

The question "when should you trust AI?" does not have a single answer that applies to every AI system and every task.

The more useful concept is appropriate reliance.

Appropriate reliance means that a person uses AI when the available evidence supports relying on it, while remaining able to recognize situations where the system should not be followed.

Several different concepts matter here.

  • AI accuracy concerns whether an AI produces correct results.
  • AI capability concerns what the system has demonstrated that it can do.
  • Human evaluation concerns whether a person can meaningfully assess the AI's output.
  • Trust concerns a person's belief that an AI system is worthy of reliance.
  • Reliance concerns whether a person actually follows or uses the AI's output.
  • Appropriate reliance concerns whether that reliance is justified under the conditions in which the AI is being used.

These concepts are related, but they are not the same thing. An AI can be accurate on a particular task without being reliable on every task. A person can trust an AI without having good evidence for that trust. And a human can remain involved in a decision without actually being able to evaluate the AI's output effectively.

The goal is therefore not maximum trust or maximum skepticism.

The goal is to make reliance match what the AI has actually demonstrated and what the human-AI workflow can support.

1. Does the AI Have Demonstrated Capability for This Task?

The first boundary is capability.

AI systems can have different strengths and weaknesses. Evidence that a system performs well in one situation does not automatically establish that it will perform equally well in another.

Bansal and colleagues (2019) examined human-AI decision making and showed why the relationship between AI capability and human reliance matters. People do not necessarily adjust their reliance perfectly to the AI's actual performance.

Dunning, Fischhoff, and Davis (2024) likewise examined how people relied on AI agents with different levels of skill. Their results showed that people benefited from highly skilled AI, but also that they could rely too heavily on a highly skilled system. At the other end, people could rely too little on a lower-skilled system.

That distinction is important.

Human reliance does not automatically match AI capability.

A strong AI can still be over-relied upon. A weaker AI can be under-relied upon.

This means that "Is this AI good?" is often a less useful question than "What has this AI demonstrated that it can do?"

Boundary: The relevant question is not whether the AI is generally good. It is what the AI has demonstrated that it can do.

This does not create a universal rule about where every AI system will succeed or fail. It means that task-specific evidence is more relevant than assuming that capability transfers automatically between tasks.

2. Can You Meaningfully Evaluate the Output?

Demonstrated capability is only part of the question.

The next question is whether the human using the AI has a meaningful way to evaluate what it produces.

Human presence alone does not guarantee effective oversight. A person can be involved in a decision while still having difficulty recognizing when an AI recommendation is incorrect.

Bansal and colleagues (2019) provide evidence that human reliance depends partly on how well people understand the AI's capabilities and limitations. If people do not have a meaningful way to judge when an AI should or should not be followed, simply putting a human in the loop does not automatically produce appropriate reliance.

Fok and Weld (2024) make a related distinction in their supporting synthesis of research on AI-advised decision making. They argue that explanations are useful for appropriate reliance to the extent that they allow a human to verify whether an AI recommendation is correct.

This leads to an important principle:

An explanation is not the same thing as evidence that lets you check an answer.

An AI can explain an answer without that explanation establishing that the answer is correct. A convincing explanation can make an output easier to understand without making it independently verified.

But this does not mean humans are simply incapable of evaluating AI.

Kelso and colleagues (2025) provide an important balancing point. Their research found that AI assistance improved people's ability to distinguish correct from incorrect eyewitness identifications in several tested conditions.

The finding matters because it shows that AI assistance can sometimes improve human evaluation rather than merely replace it.

The relevant question is therefore not simply whether a human is present.

It is whether the human has a meaningful way to evaluate the output.

Boundary: Reliance is more defensible when the human can meaningfully evaluate the AI's output rather than simply accepting an explanation as proof.

3. Is the AI's Confidence Actually Meaningful?

Some AI systems provide confidence information alongside their recommendations. That information can affect whether people decide to rely on the system.

But confidence is only useful as a guide to reliance when it is meaningfully related to correctness.

Fregosi and colleagues (2026) examined how calibrated and poorly calibrated AI confidence affected human reliance and decision outcomes. In the tested setting, well-calibrated confidence produced better outcomes than poorly calibrated confidence.

Calibration matters because poorly calibrated confidence can encourage inappropriate reliance in either direction.

If an AI expresses high confidence when its recommendation is incorrect, a person may be encouraged to accept an incorrect answer. If confidence is poorly calibrated in the other direction, people may reject useful AI recommendations.

The simple principle is:

High confidence ≠ high accuracy.

What matters is whether the confidence information has been shown to be calibrated for the system and conditions in which it is being used.

Boundary: Confidence information is more useful when it is calibrated for the relevant system and task.

The Fregosi et al. study does not establish that all AI confidence signals are calibrated. It shows that calibration can affect reliance and outcomes when confidence information is used to guide human decisions.

4. Does AI Actually Improve the Human-AI Workflow?

The final boundary shifts attention away from the AI by itself and toward the entire human-AI workflow.

The important question is not merely:

"Did the AI get the answer right?"

It is:

"Did using the AI help the human perform better at the task?"

This distinction matters because an AI system does not operate in isolation when it is being used as decision support. The final result depends on how the human and AI interact.

Kelso and colleagues (2025) provide a positive example. AI assistance improved people's ability to distinguish correct from incorrect eyewitness identifications in several tested conditions.

That finding shows that AI assistance can produce a benefit at the level of the human-AI workflow.

Dunning, Fischhoff, and Davis (2024) provide an important counterweight. Their findings showed that highly skilled AI could improve performance, while people could still rely too heavily on it. Lower-skilled AI could also be underused.

In other words, useful AI does not automatically produce optimal human-AI performance.

The question is whether the particular combination of human, AI, task, and conditions actually produces a better result.

Boundary: Rely on AI more when its use has actually demonstrated a benefit in the relevant human-AI workflow.

This does not mean AI-human collaboration is always better. It means that workflow-level performance is part of the evidence for appropriate reliance.

Evidence Strength

The evidence is strongest for narrow claims about the tasks and conditions that were actually tested.

These studies provide evidence about relationships between AI capability, human reliance, human evaluation, confidence calibration, and human-AI performance in their respective settings.

The evidence becomes weaker when those findings are turned into universal rules about AI.

The studies do not establish one universal accuracy threshold at which an AI becomes trustworthy. They also do not establish that every AI system behaves the same way, or that every modern large language model has the same properties as the systems tested in these studies.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore conditional rather than absolute: reliance should be informed by what the particular system has demonstrated, what the human can evaluate, how meaningful its confidence information is, and whether the human-AI workflow actually performs better.

Claims to Avoid

The evidence does not justify several broad claims that can sound stronger than the research actually is.

  • "AI cannot be trusted."
  • "AI should always be verified."
  • "Humans always overtrust AI."
  • "AI is more reliable than humans."
  • "AI is less reliable than humans."
  • "AI explanations prove correctness."
  • "AI confidence tells you whether an answer is correct."
  • "If an AI is accurate, all of its answers are reliable."
  • "AI should never be used in high-stakes decisions."
  • "AI should always be used as a second opinion."

Each of these statements turns conditional evidence into a universal rule.

Conclusion: When Should You Trust AI?

So, when should you trust AI?

The evidence does not provide one universal point at which an AI becomes "trustworthy."

Instead, appropriate reliance depends on several conditions.

  • Demonstrated capability: Has the AI shown that it can perform the particular task?
  • Meaningful evaluation: Can the human meaningfully evaluate the output rather than simply accepting it?
  • Calibrated confidence: When confidence information is available, has it been shown to be meaningfully calibrated?
  • Workflow benefit: Has using the AI actually improved performance in the relevant human-AI workflow?

These conditions do not create a formula that tells you exactly how much to trust every AI system.

They provide a better way to think about the question.

The issue is not whether AI deserves trust in the abstract. It is whether reliance on this AI, for this task, under these conditions, is supported by evidence.

Trust is not something you give an AI once and for all.
